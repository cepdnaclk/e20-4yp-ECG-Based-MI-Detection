Quality Over Quantity:
The Impact of Diagnostic Certainty of Data in Deep Learning for ECG Analysis

We show that training on high-certainty, cardiologist-validated ECG labels produces better-calibrated and more clinically interpretable myocardial infarction (MI) detection models than adding larger amounts of uncertain labels — across two architectures.

About the Project

Why Label Quality Matters

Most ECG deep learning studies report accuracy and AUROC but rarely ask whether a model's predicted probability of MI can be trusted. We ask: does the diagnostic certainty of training labels matter more than dataset size?

Binary MI Detection

Classifying 12-lead ECGs as Myocardial Infarction (MI) vs. Pure Normal using PTB-XL, whose cardiologist-assigned SCP codes carry diagnostic confidence scores (0–100%).

⚖

Controlled Comparison

Every variant shares the same 4,451 Pure Normal training ECGs and the same certain-only validation and test sets. Only the certainty of the MI training labels changes.

◉

Calibration First

Alongside standard classification metrics we measure Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Brier score — whether a predicted 80% MI risk really means 80%.

⚙

Clinical Interpretability

Subclass-aware Integrated Gradients on both architectures checks whether models attend to the ECG leads that match the infarct territory (anterior vs. inferior MI).

Dataset Design

Four Training Variants, One Question

From human-validated PTB-XL records we defined three groups — Pure Normal (NORM at 100%, no other statements), Certain MI (maximum MI confidence = 100%) and Uncertain MI (maximum MI confidence < 100%) — and built four training sets that differ only in their MI cases.

Dataset MI Type MI Cases Normal Cases Total Train Label Certainty
Dataset A Certain MI 1,194 4,451 5,645 100% certain
Dataset C All MI 2,387 (1,194 + 1,193) 4,451 6,838 Mixed (~50% certain) — maximal data
Dataset D Size-matched mix 1,194 (597 + 597) 4,451 5,645 50% certain — same size as A
Dataset B Uncertain MI 1,193 4,451 5,644 <100% only — exploratory

The primary comparison is A vs. D (label certainty at fixed training size); C shows the effect of adding uncertain data. B contains no confirmed MI and is excluded from the primary analysis.

Patient-Wise Splitting

Official PTB-XL stratified folds (1–8 train, 9 validation, 10 test) keep every patient in exactly one split, preventing information leakage.

Shared Certain-Only Evaluation

Uncertain MI is removed from validation and test. Validation: 977 ECGs (694 normal, 283 certain MI). Test: 971 ECGs (703 normal, 268 certain MI) — identical for every model.

Preprocessing

10 s recordings at 100 Hz (1000 samples × 12 leads), 0.5 Hz Butterworth high-pass for baseline wander, and lead-wise Z-score normalisation fit on training data.

Key Results

Certain Labels Give Trustworthy Probabilities

All six models were evaluated on the same certain-only test set (971 ECGs). Training on diagnostically certain MI labels (Dataset A) produced the best-calibrated models for both architectures.

Certainty Improves Calibration

Dataset A gives the lowest ECE for both models — 0.0184 (CNN-LSTM) and 0.0339 (Mamba-2). Including uncertain labels raises it to 0.0233 / 0.0617 (C) and 0.0465 / 0.1377 (D).

More Data Helps Sensitivity

All-MI training (Dataset C) gives Mamba-2 its strongest recall (96.27%, only 10 missed MI) and AUPRC (0.9814) — but at the cost of calibration.

Replacing Certain Labels Hurts

At the same training size, swapping half the certain MI cases for uncertain ones (Dataset D) produces the most false alarms (48 for CNN-LSTM, 66 for Mamba-2) and the worst calibration.

XAI Validates Clinical Relevance

Anterior MI attributions focus on V1–V3 in every configuration. For inferior MI, Datasets A and C keep an inferior lead (III or aVF) in the top 3; Dataset D does not.

Deployment Guidance

Dataset A suits probability-based risk stratification. Dataset C suits high-sensitivity screening and triage. Dataset D is consistently unfavourable.
